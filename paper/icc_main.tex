\title{Beyond Chronological Splits: A Deployment-Causality and\\
Falsifiability Contract for Learning-Assisted\\
Satellite Communication Software}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Submission}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Learning-assisted satellite communication software is routinely validated with a
chronological train/validation/test split. Ordering, however, is only one of the
properties a deployment claim rests on. We show that a pipeline can satisfy
chronological splitting exactly and still be uninterpretable, because ordering
constrains neither whether a past-dated quantity was \emph{available} at the decision
instant, nor which rows \emph{exist}, nor what a learner's hidden state has already
observed. We formalise six protected objects---decision time, feature availability,
row membership, label closure, state channels and statistical units---as an executable
experiment contract in four mechanically checkable layers, and release
\emph{Orbit-Evidence}, a dependency-light implementation. In a fault-injection
evaluation over two pipelines, eighteen fault classes and three deterministic
environments, chronological checks detect $2/18$ classes while the contract detects
$18/18$, including four mutations specified before their detectors were written, with
zero clean-path false positives and a sub-\qty{0.2}{\second} sweep suitable for
per-commit use. Two case studies show chronology-compliant experiments halted by a
contract rule before any conclusion was drawn. We make no claim about physical-layer
performance; the contribution is the contract and its fault-detection evidence.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
experiment validity, data leakage, reproducibility, non-terrestrial networks,

\section{Introduction}

Learning components are increasingly proposed as assistants to physical models in
satellite and non-terrestrial communication software: correcting a propagated orbital
prediction, adapting a link parameter, or deciding whether a learned branch should
override a model-based default~\cite{lin2021ntn}. Because such systems are
inherently temporal, the community's standard remedy against optimistic evaluation is
the chronological split: train on the past, validate on the near future, test on the
far future~\cite{bergmeir2012crossval}.

Chronological splitting is necessary. Our claim is that it is not sufficient, and that
the gap is not a matter of degree but of kind. A chronological split constrains the
\emph{order} of quantities already present in a dataset. It is silent on at least
three further questions. First, \emph{availability}: a quantity may be dated in the
past and yet not have been published, so a system could not have held it. Catalogue-fed
pipelines carry at least two clocks---when an item describes, and when it was
released---and a difference of two past timestamps passes every ordering test while
still being unknowable at the decision instant. Second, \emph{membership}: which rows
exist can itself be decided by later data, in both directions, and a split cannot
examine rows that were never created. Third, \emph{hidden state}: a split governs rows,
whereas leakage can travel through a scaler, a coefficient vector, a tracker's latent
state, selection metadata, or an admission bit---none of which is a column.

These channels are not exotic. They are the operational reality of any online-learning
system fed by a published data source, and the satellite case makes them unusually
crisp: element sets have an epoch and a publication time that differ by hours,
retrospective labels arrive only when the catalogue chooses to publish, and a link
budget is meaningful only for geometry that can actually carry a link.

We therefore propose treating experimental validity as an \emph{executable contract}
rather than a protocol description. Each contract rule protects a named object, states
a violation, and is enforced by a mechanical check that identifies the rule it broke.
Following mutation-testing practice~\cite{jia2011mutation}, we evaluate the contract by
injecting faults and measuring detection, including mutations withheld from the
detectors' authors.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A threat model and contract.} Six protected objects and the
deployment-causality violations each admits, organised as four mechanically checkable
layers: availability (L1), physical and scheduling validity (L2), model-state causality
(L3), and independence and reproducibility (L4). Nineteen rules, Table~\ref{tab:rules}.
\item \textbf{Orbit-Evidence, an executable implementation.} A dependency-light
toolkit (710 lines in four modules plus a 399-line two-sided test suite,
\texttt{numpy} only) providing visible-pass scheduling, a
freeze-then-label transmission registry, reference-ensemble labelling with published
uncertainty, a six-channel mutation canary, and seed and provenance controls. Every
detector carries a two-sided test that fails on a broken fixture and passes on the
clean path.
\item \textbf{A fault-injection evaluation with held-out mutations.} Fourteen
development faults and four mutations specified before their detectors existed,
injected into two pipelines across three deterministic environments, with detection
rate, clean-path false positives, determinism and continuous-integration runtime;
plus two case studies of chronology-compliant experiments halted by a rule.
\end{enumerate}

We claim nothing about radio-frequency or packet-level performance. No learned
communication method is shown here to work or to fail; the object of study is the
experiment, not the model.

\section{Threat Model and Experiment Contract}

\subsection{Decision time and the six protected objects}

Let $t_d$ be the \emph{decision instant}: the moment at which a deployed system must
act using only state it already holds. A deployment claim is interpretable only if
every quantity entering the decision was obtainable before $t_d$. We call the
following six objects \emph{protected}; Fig.~\ref{fig:contract} shows their
relationship in time.

\emph{Feature availability.} A feature must be computable from state available at
$t_d$. The adversarial case is not a future-dated feature---that is caught by
ordering---but a feature built from two past timestamps, one of which belongs to an
item published after $t_d$.

\emph{Availability clock.} An item's descriptive timestamp and its publication
timestamp are distinct clocks. Selection must use publication. Conflating them admits
data the system could not have held, and the error is silent because both values lie
in the past.

\emph{Row membership.} Later information may change a label's status, value,
uncertainty or closure time. It must never change whether a row exists. Violations
occur in both directions: rows dropped when a later reference fails to appear, and rows
created when a schedule's extent is derived from the data on hand rather than declared.

\emph{Label closure.} A retrospective label becomes usable only at a closure time
$t_c$. Its use before $t_c$ is an ordering violation and \emph{is} caught by a split---the
one class in our taxonomy for which that is true, drawn dotted in
Fig.~\ref{fig:contract} for contrast. The harder failure is when the \emph{probability}
of a label existing depends on the covariate under study, which is a missingness property
that no split addresses.

\emph{State channels.} Beyond the feature tensor, information can enter through the
scaler, model coefficients, a tracker's latent state, selected-model metadata, or the
gate bit. A canary scoped to features misses five of these six.

\emph{Statistical units.} Replicates of one physical event are not independent
observations, and aggregating at some level does not establish that the level chosen is
the exchangeable one.

\subsection{Four layers}

L1 protects availability, the clock distinction, closure and membership. L2 protects
physical validity: a schedule must lie inside geometry that can carry the link, the
target must lie between a resolution floor and a plausibility ceiling, declared
relations must match implementations, and hidden state must not diverge without bound.
L3 protects model-state causality through effective mutation canaries and a negative
control. L4 protects fold chronology, paired randomness, repeated-measure aggregation,
seed hygiene and provenance completeness. Table~\ref{tab:rules} lists all rules.

Two design commitments make the contract usable. Every violation raises an exception
carrying its \emph{rule identifier}, so a finding names what it broke rather than
surfacing a bare assertion failure. And every detector must be demonstrated to fail on
a deliberately broken fixture: a check that cannot go red is not evidence, a point we
return to in Section~\ref{sec:cases}.

\subsection{Relation to existing practice}

Leakage taxonomies in data mining catalogue how information about a target reaches a
model illegitimately~\cite{kaufman2012leakage}, and recent surveys document its
prevalence in machine-learning-based science~\cite{kapoor2023leakage}. Our threat model
is narrower and operational: it asks not only whether a quantity is legitimate but
whether the deployed system could have \emph{held} it, which turns a modelling concern
into a scheduling and provenance concern. Time-series evaluation work establishes when
ordered splitting is valid~\cite{bergmeir2012crossval}; we take that as given and
address the properties it does not cover. The hidden-state channels we guard are
instances of the configuration and state coupling identified as technical debt in
production learning systems~\cite{sculley2015debt}, made checkable here. Our evaluation
method is mutation testing~\cite{jia2011mutation} applied to an experimental protocol
rather than to program logic, and the provenance manifests follow the documentation
discipline urged for datasets~\cite{gebru2021datasheets}. The orbital element
conventions are standard~\cite{vallado2006revisiting}.

\section{Orbit-Evidence Toolkit}

\emph{Visible-pass scheduler.} Transmissions are \emph{generated} from intervals
predicted by the state the system holds, located by a coarse scan and refined by
bisection to both threshold crossings. Both crossings are refined to the
above-threshold side of the final bracket, so no scheduled sample can fall below the
mask through refinement error. Elevation uses the geodetic normal, not the geocentric
radius. The operative visibility criterion, including the minimum pass duration, is
declared rather than left implicit.

\emph{Freeze-then-label registry.} Rows are assembled and hashed before any label
source is consulted, against an \emph{absolute declared} window. Deriving either bound
from the data on hand makes the schedule a property of the download; the falsifiable
form of the invariant compares a registry built from full data against one built from
truncated data using the same declared window.

\emph{Reference ensemble.} Where a label must be assembled from several later
observations, the toolkit canonicalises them deterministically, takes the median, and
publishes a median-absolute-deviation uncertainty and a closure time. Rows whose spread
is large are marked and \emph{retained}: deleting them selects on the outcome. The
module documents its own limitation---when members share a systematic error, a mutual
deviation statistic cannot see it---and exposes a split-half diagnostic so the
published uncertainty is treated as a lower bound.

\subsection{Two rules that are not checks on values}

Most contract rules inspect a quantity. Two inspect a \emph{relation between runs}, and
we state them precisely because they are the least obvious and, in our evaluation, the
two for which no detector previously existed.

\emph{Provenance completeness} (\rulename{L4.6}) cannot be verified by examining a
manifest, since a manifest is silent about what it omits. It is verifiable
differentially. Let $R$ be a run producing output digest $o(R)$ and manifest hash
$h(R)$. Over a set of input variations $\{c_i\}$,
\begin{equation}
\exists\, i,j:\; o_i \neq o_j \;\wedge\; h_i = h_j
\;\Longrightarrow\; \text{manifest incomplete},
\label{eq:prov}
\end{equation}
writing $o_i = o(R_{c_i})$ and $h_i = h(R_{c_i})$,
since two runs that differ in output must differ in provenance. The converse is not
an error: identical outputs under differing manifests is merely redundant. This
asymmetry is what makes the rule checkable without enumerating every possible input.

\emph{Statistical unit selection} (\rulename{L4.7}) asks not whether replicates were
aggregated but whether the level they were aggregated \emph{to} is the exchangeable one.
Aggregating correctly at some level says nothing about that level's adequacy. Given
values $y$, chosen unit identifiers $u$, and the next coarser grouping $g$, we aggregate
$\bar{y}_u$ and then measure the intraclass correlation of $\bar{y}_u$ \emph{within} $g$:
\begin{equation}
\rho\!\left(\bar{y}_u \mid g\right) > \rho_{\max}
\;\Longrightarrow\; \text{unit finer than exchangeable.}
\label{eq:unit}
\end{equation}
Residual correlation at the coarser level means the chosen unit shares state with its
neighbours, so intervals computed over it are optimistic. A pipeline can pass a
conventional replicate check and fail this one, which is precisely the case we injected.

Two properties of the estimator are load-bearing and were not obvious to us. It must be an
unbiased variance-components ICC: a raw between-over-total scatter ratio has null
expectation $\approx 1/m$ for $m$ observations per group, so any fixed threshold below that
value halts on correct designs. And the rule must \emph{decline to judge} when it cannot
estimate---below a declared minimum number of coarser groups it reports indeterminate and
raises nothing, since a rule that halts on an unestimable statistic is worse than no rule.
We found both only after making the clean fixture non-degenerate; the degenerate clean path
had been silently short-circuiting the check.

\emph{Mutation canaries.} Six channels are probed. The toolkit separates two questions
that are easily conflated: whether a channel is \emph{guarded}, and whether the
mutation used to probe it was \emph{effective}. An inert mutation changes nothing and
can be misread as an undetectable leak; \rulename{L3.2} exists to catch that.

\emph{Provenance and seeds.} Seeds live in three disjoint namespaces---burned, debug,
evaluation. A seed whose outcomes have been inspected is burned and can never re-enter
evaluation. Compared conditions draw one seed per invariant cell so that only the
intervention differs, and pairing is asserted on the realised arrays rather than
inferred from the derivation rule.

\section{Fault-Injection Evaluation}

\subsection{Design}

Two pipelines are used as minimal deterministic fixtures. \textsc{Case A} exercises a
retrospective-label chain: held state, visible-pass scheduling, frozen registry, later
reference construction, closure. \textsc{Case B} exercises a learning and gating chain:
physics baseline, optional learned branch, validation selection, frozen
model/scaler/gate state, held-out deployment. They carry no physical claim; they exist
to exercise rules.

We inject fourteen \emph{development} faults, drawn from defects that motivated the
detectors, and four \emph{held-out} mutations whose definitions were frozen before the
corresponding rules were written and whose outcomes were not inspected until after the
rules were fixed. Two of the four---incomplete provenance and a statistical unit chosen
at the wrong nesting level---had \emph{no} predecessor detector of any kind, so they
test whether a general rule can be written for a defect it was not designed against.
Each condition runs in three environments differing only in pseudo-random generator
family.

\subsection{Results}

Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage} gives the coverage matrix; Fig.~\ref{fig:cases} shows where each
case study was halted.

The chronological baseline is \emph{implemented and executed}, not reasoned about: three
checks assert that fold time-extents are ordered and disjoint, that no row sits in a fold
whose span it does not belong to, and that no label used at a decision point postdates it.
It runs inside the same sweep, over the same fixtures, in the same three environments. We
stress that it is not a competing detector suite: chronological splitting is a
\emph{protocol property} of intentionally limited scope, and the comparison measures how
much of the fault space that property covers, not tool against tool.

Measured, it detects $2/18$ fault classes---invalid label-closure timing and a violated
fold order, precisely the two that \emph{are} ordering properties---and fires on no clean
path. The remaining sixteen are invisible to ordering by construction. The contract
detects $18/18$ classes in all three environments, $54/54$ condition-environment cells.

All four held-out mutations were detected, in every environment, with \emph{zero}
correction loops drawn from a pre-registered budget of two. Clean paths produced no
false positives, and clean verdicts were identical across the three environments.
Three faults additionally raise \rulename{L3.2}: when a state channel is unguarded, the
mutation aimed at it is also inert, which is a true positive rather than noise.

The full sweep---nineteen conditions per environment, being the eighteen fault classes
plus one clean path---over both pipelines and three environments runs
in under \qty{0.2}{\second} on a single core of a commodity laptop---about
\qty{3}{\milli\second} per condition---with
\texttt{numpy} as the only dependency, so it is affordable as a per-commit gate rather
than a periodic audit.

\subsection{A false positive we did not design}

The first matrix run reported three clean-path false positives, all of
\rulename{L4.4}, one per environment. The cause was the fixture, not the detector: the
clean pipeline declared it was about to execute a seed still present in its own
evaluation namespace, which is a genuine violation. We repaired the fixture, changed no
detector, and preserved both records. We report this because the sequence matters: had
we adjusted the rule instead, the reported clean-path false-positive rate would have
been achieved by weakening the contract.

\section{Case Studies and Lessons}
\label{sec:cases}

Both case studies are software pipelines that were built with chronological splitting
and passed it. We report which rule halted each and why ordering did not, and no
model-performance quantity from either.

\subsection{Retrospective-label pipeline}

A system holds a stale orbital element set and predicts open-loop; labels are formed by
comparison against a later, better-determined element set from the same catalogue;
folds are split chronologically by reference time. Three defects were present. A feature
was the interval between the held element's epoch and the \emph{reference} element's
epoch---a difference of two past timestamps, and therefore ordering-consistent, but
containing an item the system had not received (\rulename{L1.1}). Row membership
depended on the future catalogue, because a transmission entered the dataset only if a
qualifying later element was subsequently published (\rulename{L1.4}). And label
availability was coupled to the covariate under study, because a publication outage
simultaneously makes the held element stale and removes the elements needed to label
that staleness. An intermediate version additionally scheduled transmissions on a clock
grid rather than inside predicted-visible geometry (\rulename{L2.1}).

Ordering cannot see any of these: none of them violates ordering. The consequence is
that a reported improvement could not be attributed to information the system would
actually hold, so the defects would invalidate any deployment interpretation. Under the
third defect the comparison is not merely biased but undetermined, because the baseline
is not pinned down more tightly than the effect sought. The contract prevented formal
evaluation from proceeding.

\subsection{Learning and gating pipeline}

A physics baseline, an optional learned branch, selection on a chronologically prior
validation window, an admission decision frozen before the held-out window, and
fallback to the baseline. The feature tensor was clean and verified so by perturbation.
Nevertheless future information entered through a non-feature state channel: a stateful
tracker continued to advance at refresh events falling inside the held-out window, so
the learner kept observing outcomes after its own freeze (\rulename{L3.1}). Separately,
the negative control exposed undeclared deterministic signal---a rate frozen at one
epoch while the reference continued to evolve leaves a residual that is a deterministic
function of the covariate, hence learnable, with the injected effect at exactly zero
(\rulename{L3.3}). Compared conditions also failed to share a realisation, because the
condition label had entered the seed derivation (\rulename{L4.2}).

A row-level split governs rows and so cannot constrain model state; the tracker's
update was chronologically forward at every step and still wrong, because it crossed
the freeze. The control involves no leakage at all---its rows are perfectly ordered and
its target is simply not null. Blind evaluation seeds remained unexecuted after
qualification failed, so no result exists to be misread.

\subsection{The lesson we did not expect}

Across four independent review cycles of these two pipelines, five successive
corrective changes each introduced a new defect, and in every cycle the new defect was
found by an independent check rather than by the author's own review. We draw a
procedural conclusion rather than a technical one: a fix is itself a change and carries
its own defect rate. Budget an independent adversarial check per fix, and fix a stop
count in advance. Relatedly, three checks in the original suites were found to be
incapable of failing---one compared two arrays built from the same object, one pinned
the very parameter whose influence it tested, and one attached no threshold to the
quantity it reported. Every detector in this work is therefore required to be
demonstrated red on a broken fixture before it is trusted.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}

This is a software-methodology contribution. We validate no radio-frequency behaviour,
no packet-level outcome, and no learned communication method; the fixtures are
deterministic constructions for exercising rules, not simulators, and no physical
conclusion should be drawn from them. We make no completeness claim: the contract
covers six protected objects we found necessary, and the existence of further channels
is likely rather than excluded---our own evaluation added two rules for propositions
that had no detector until they were sought. The held-out mutation count is small, and a
detection rate over eighteen fault classes bounds nothing about faults nobody has
conceived. Finally, the two case studies come from one programme; the threat model
generalises by argument, not by demonstration across independent projects.

What the evidence supports is narrow and, we think, useful. Chronological splitting
addresses ordering and only ordering; on our matrix it detects $2$ of $18$ fault
classes, and the sixteen it misses include every availability, membership, hidden-state
and unit-selection defect we injected. An executable contract detected all eighteen,
including four mutations written before their detectors, at a cost small enough to run
on every commit. The natural next step is to apply the same machinery where labels come
from measurement rather than from a published catalogue, which removes the
covariate-coupled censoring that halted our first case study; that application is
future work and is not claimed here.
